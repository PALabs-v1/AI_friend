# R2: Affect, body state and neuromodulation

**Scope and grading rule.** This is a literature/code audit for the affect track, not a claim that an engineered scalar state is a biological hormone or emotion. “Faithful” means the source model's computational structure is present, including required variables and time semantics. A source-inspired mechanism with explicit engineering units and limits is a deliberate simplification. A label is not evidence of a model. Model parameters below are computational parameters unless the text explicitly identifies a measured physical quantity.

## 1. Models

### 1.1 Dimensional affect: core affect and PAD

Russell's core affect is a continuously changing, consciously accessible state on two bipolar dimensions: pleasant–unpleasant (valence) and energized–enervated (arousal). It is not a categorical emotion label and it has no canonical equation, biological unit, or fixed numeric bounds. A cause-free core affect can be experienced as mood; an emotion episode occurs when core affect is attributed to an object or event. See James A. Russell (2003), “Core Affect and the Psychological Construction of Emotion,” *Psychological Review* 110(1):145–172, pp. 145–147, [DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.110.1.145](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.110.1.145), [opened author-hosted PDF](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~jhoey/teaching/cs886-affect/papers/Russell-CoreAffect-PsychRev03.pdf).

Mehrabian and Russell's PAD adds dominance (dominant–submissive, perceived control/potency) to pleasure and arousal for describing affective reactions to environments. PAD is a measurement/modeling space, not a universal physiological coordinate system. The scale depends on instrument and normalization: semantic differential terms and Self-Assessment Manikin (SAM) pictograms are common; SAM uses discrete ratings (often 9 response positions), while many affective-computing datasets normalize valence/arousal to [-1,1] and dominance to an instrument-specific range. Neutral is a scale midpoint, not necessarily a person's long-run set point. There is no biological “natural” bound at -1/+1. Sources: Albert Mehrabian & James A. Russell (1974), *An Approach to Environmental Psychology*, MIT Press, [publisher page](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262130905/an-approach-to-environmental-psychology/); Margaret M. Bradley & Peter J. Lang (1994), “Measuring Emotion: The Self-Assessment Manikin and the Semantic Differential,” *Journal of Behavior Therapy and Experimental Psychiatry* 25(1):49–59, [DOI 10.1016/0005-7916(94)90063-9](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7916(94)90063-9), [opened paper PDF](https://edwp.educ.msu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2020/06/Bradley_1994_MeasuringEmotionTheSelfassessment.pdf).

**Implication:** core affect is 2D; PAD's dominance should be measured/estimated as control appraisal and not inferred from arousal. Code bounds such as valence [-1,1], arousal [0,1], dominance [0,1] are engineering scale choices. If benchmark labels use SAM, map its midpoint and range explicitly and retain raw instrument ratings.

### 1.2 Emotion, mood, inertia, adaptation

There is no evidence for a single fixed “emotion half-life.” Russell separates an attributed, event-linked emotion episode from free-floating mood. In two daily-report experience-sampling studies, Verduyn, Delvaux, Van Coillie, Tuerlinckx, and Van Mechelen recorded fear, anger, joy, gratitude, and sadness for 14 days. Study 1 (59 students; 15-minute duration bins, right-censored above 120 min) found median episode durations of fear 16 min, anger 22 min, joy 26 min. Study 2 (43 participants; 10-minute bins up to 60 min, then right-censored) found 80% of episodes ended within 30 min and about 90% within 60 min. More intense episodes, more important eliciting situations, and renewed physical or mental exposure lasted longer. Those are episodic durations from daily reports, not a mood decay constant. Philippe Verduyn et al. (2009), “Predicting the Duration of Emotional Experience: Two Experience Sampling Studies,” *Emotion* 9(1):83–91, pp. 83–91, [DOI 10.1037/a0014610](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014610), [opened author PDF](https://ppw.kuleuven.be/cscp/documents/delvaux/verduyn-delvaux-van-coillie-tuerlinckx-van.pdf/@@download/file/Verduyn%20Delvaux%20Van%20Coillie%20Tuerlinckx%20%26%20Van%20Mechelen%20%282009%29%20Predicting%20the%20duration%20of%20emotional%20experience_two%20experience%20sampling%20studies.pdf).

Emotional inertia is operationalized as lagged within-person dependence, not duration in seconds independent of sampling interval. In Kuppens, Allen, & Sheeber (2010), 79 university students rated eight emotions on 0–100 sliders at 10 random prompts/day for 14 days (82% response; roughly 84 min between prompts). Mean lag-1 regression slopes were 0.20–0.33 across emotions; self-esteem moderated the slope for happy (-0.07), excited (-0.08), anxious (-0.14), and depressed (-0.11) ratings. These are slopes in the authors' group-mean-centered multilevel model, not universal autocorrelation constants. A separate lab study coded 141 adolescents' affective behavior during parent interactions at 5-s lags: depression increased logistic autoregressive slopes for happy (+0.21), angry (+0.26), and dysphoric (+0.24) behavior, especially in conflict/reminiscence rather than positive interactions. Do not compare those logistic slopes to the hour-scale self-report coefficients. Peter Kuppens, Nicholas B. Allen, & Lisa B. Sheeber (2010), “Emotional Inertia and Psychological Maladjustment,” *Psychological Science* 21(7):984–991, Table 1 and Study 2, [DOI 10.1177/0956797610372634](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610372634), [opened full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2901421/).

DynAffect formalizes core affect as a continuous-time mean-reverting stochastic process. The source's one-dimensional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck transition is:

\[
 d\theta_p(t)=\beta_p[\mu_p-\theta_p(t)]dt+\sigma_p dW_p(t),\qquad Y_p(t)=\theta_p(t)+\epsilon_p(t).
\]

Here \(\theta_p\) is latent valence or arousal, \(Y_p\) its observation, \(\mu_p\) the person's home base (instrument units), \(\beta_p>0\) attractor strength (inverse of the paper's time unit), \(\sigma_p\) process variability (affect units/√time), \(W\) Wiener noise, and \(\epsilon\) measurement error. Parameters are person-fitted; there are no universal defaults. Under this model, perturbation retention after elapsed \(\Delta t\) is \(e^{-\beta\Delta t}\), and the conditional half-life is \(\ln 2/\beta\). In its two ESM samples (N=79, 10/day, about 84 min; and N=60, 50/day, about 17 min), reported population mean attractor strengths were valence/arousal β=.026/.025 and .043/.049 in the paper's time units (Tables 2 and 5). Measurement interval changed estimates; the fitted β values should not be moved into production without preserving the time unit and scale. The authors explicitly note stable-attractor, symmetric-return and no explicit time-varying appraisal/input limitations. Peter Kuppens, Zita Oravecz, & Francis Tuerlinckx (2010), “Feelings Change: Accounting for Individual Differences in the Temporal Dynamics of Affect,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 99(6):1042–1060, model and Tables 2/5, [DOI 10.1037/a0020962](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020962), [opened primary PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~zoravecz/bayes/data/Articles/Kuppens2010FCAFI.pdf).

Set-point return is useful as a compact short-horizon dynamic, but a fixed neutral set point and universal recovery speed are not established. Longitudinal well-being work finds differing baselines and incomplete adaptation for some events/people. Treat the mood attractor as a per-person fitted behavioral parameter, allow it to drift only under validated long-run evidence, and keep life-satisfaction adaptation studies separate from minute/hour affect dynamics.

### 1.3 Appraisal models and regulation

- **Scherer's Component Process Model (CPM):** emotion emerges from synchronized changes in cognitive appraisal, action tendency, physiology, expression, and feeling. Stimulus Evaluation Checks proceed through relevance (novelty, intrinsic pleasantness, goal relevance), implications (goal conduciveness, cause/agency, urgency), coping potential (control/power/adjustment), and normative significance (internal/external standards). The model predicts differentiated component patterns; it does not prescribe one canonical numeric appraisal-to-PAD equation or universal parameter defaults. Sequence is conceptual/functional, not a universal millisecond latency chart. Klaus R. Scherer (2009), “The Dynamic Architecture of Emotion: Evidence for the Component Process Model,” *Cognition & Emotion* 23(7):1307–1351, [DOI 10.1080/02699930902928969](https://doi.org/10.1080/02699930902928969); and Scherer (2001), “Appraisal Considered as a Process of Multilevel Sequential Checking,” in *Appraisal Processes in Emotion*, pp. 92–120, [DOI 10.1093/oso/9780195130072.003.0005](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780195130072.003.0005).
- **OCC:** Ortony, Clore, & Collins is a structural taxonomy, not a complete algorithm. Event desirability elicits pleased/displeased reactions; agent actions are assessed for praiseworthiness; objects for appealingness. It names 22 emotion types and intensity variables, but supplies no universal numeric range or single formula. Numeric rule thresholds are implementer-selected. Andrew Ortony, Gerald L. Clore, & Allan Collins (1988), *The Cognitive Structure of Emotions*, Cambridge University Press, [publisher catalog/chapter](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/A42C0763DCFB4AF678BF0F26EA422085/9781108844246c3_49-70.pdf/appraisal-and-the-value-system-and-primary-sources-of-intensity.pdf).
- **EMA:** Gratch & Marsella represent beliefs/plans as appraisal frames with desirability D, likelihood L, causal attribution and coping/control appraisals. For fear/distress, Table 3 gives intensity \(I=|D(p)L(p)|\); for hope/joy it gives \(I=D(p)L(p)\) when D>0. D is utility-derived desirability, L probability; both are model-scaled, not physical units. Realized/anticipated outcomes are distinguished by likelihood assumptions. EMA sums same-label appraisal-frame intensities and uses a sigmoid mood component; mood also biases frame intensity/selection. No one-to-one continuous PAD mapping is prescribed. Jonathan Gratch & Stacy Marsella (2004), “A Domain-Independent Framework for Modeling Emotion,” *Cognitive Systems Research* 5(4):269–306, Table 3, [DOI 10.1016/j.cogsys.2004.02.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogsys.2004.02.002), [opened primary PDF](https://people.ict.usc.edu/~gratch/GratchMarsellaCOGSYS04.pdf); Stacy C. Marsella & Jonathan Gratch (2009), “EMA: A Process Model of Appraisal Dynamics,” *Cognitive Systems Research* 10(1):70–90, [DOI 10.1016/j.cogsys.2008.03.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogsys.2008.03.005), [author PDF](https://www.ccs.neu.edu/~marsella/publications/pdf/MarsellaCSR09.pdf).
- **WASABI:** a PAD-space simulation with primary/secondary emotion and mood, and mass–spring return dynamics. The manual's update is \(F(t)=-d x(t-1), a(t)=F(t)/m, v(t)=v(t-1)+a(t)\Delta t, s(t)=s(t-1)+v(t)\Delta t\). Here x/s are displacement, d spring constant, m mass, v velocity, a acceleration. Separate emotion/mood constants are simulation parameters, not human-fitted defaults. Christian Becker-Asano (2008), *WASABI: Affect Simulation for Agents with Believable Interactivity*, PhD thesis, Bielefeld University, [author PDF](https://gki.informatik.uni-freiburg.de/papers/becker-asano-phdthesis2008.pdf); [manual](https://www.becker-asano.de/index.php/research/wasabi/78-wasabi-manual?showall=1).
- **FAtiMA:** modular architecture that separates appraisal derivation from affect derivation; OCC-like rules/thresholds are configurable. It is a host for competing theories, not a single validated formula. João Dias, Samuel Mascarenhas, & Ana Paiva (2014), “FAtiMA Modular: Towards an Agent Architecture with a Generic Appraisal Framework,” *Lecture Notes in Computer Science* 8750:44–56, [DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-12973-0_3](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-12973-0_3).

These models are complementary but not interchangeable: OCC categorizes eliciting conditions, CPM models dynamic components/checks, EMA computes belief/planning appraisals and coping, WASABI supplies an affect-space dynamical simulation, FAtiMA supplies plug-in architecture. There is no empirical consensus equation converting every appraisal vector to PAD. Any AI_friend mapping should identify its chosen theory and be treated as a calibrated simplification.

Gross's process model places regulation at situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change (reappraisal), and response modulation (suppression). Reappraisal changes the interpretation before a response is fully formed; expressive suppression targets outward response after emotion onset. Neither is a generic constant multiplier on mood. In a 120-participant disgusting-film experiment, reappraisal reduced reported disgust and expressive behavior; suppression reduced expression but not felt experience and increased sympathetic activation. James J. Gross (1998), “The Emerging Field of Emotion Regulation: An Integrative Review,” *Review of General Psychology* 2:271–299, [author PDF](https://emotion.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/1353/2021/11/Gross-1998-The-Emerging-Field-of-Emotion-Regulation-An-Integrative-Review.pdf); Gross (1998), “Antecedent- and Response-Focused Emotion Regulation: Divergent Consequences for Experience, Expression, and Physiology,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 74(1):224–237, [DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.74.1.224](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.74.1.224).

### 1.4 Neuromodulators and bodily time courses

**Dopamine and reward prediction error.** The standard discounted TD(0) update is \(\delta_t=r_{t+1}+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)\), with \(V(s_t)\leftarrow V(s_t)+\alpha\delta_t\); Doya gives the corresponding TD error/value update in §2, pp. 496–498 (his displayed episodic form omits γ). Reward and value share task utility units; γ is dimensionless discount per transition, α is dimensionless learning rate. Neither has universal biological default. Schultz, Dayan, & Montague's macaque evidence shows response to unexpected reward, transfer to its predictive cue after learning, little response to expected reward, and a dip at omitted expected reward, consistent with signed TD errors. It is a teaching signal hypothesis, not an equation for dopamine concentration or pleasure. Wolfram Schultz, Peter Dayan, & P. Read Montague (1997), “A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward,” *Science* 275(5306):1593–1599, [DOI 10.1126/science.275.5306.1593](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.275.5306.1593), [opened author PDF](https://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/~dayan/papers/sdm97.pdf).

**Noradrenaline and adaptive gain.** Aston-Jones & Cohen propose two locus-coeruleus modes: moderate tonic activity plus task-related phasic bursts supports task focus/exploitation; elevated tonic activity with reduced phasic selectivity supports disengagement/exploration. The behavioral relationship to tonic activation is inverted-U shaped. A simple neural transfer function is logistic activation \(1/(1+e^{-g u})\) (gain g times net input u; Aston-Jones & Cohen, Fig. 3, p. 409); this is an illustrative computational mapping, not a transmitter-concentration equation or fitted default. Their Eq. 1, p. 430, gives engagement as `[1−logistic(short-term utility)] × logistic(long-term utility)`, where `logistic(x)=1/(1+e^(−x))`; utility scales are illustrative, not physical units. Some theory components are explicitly provisional. Gary Aston-Jones & Jonathan D. Cohen (2005), “An Integrative Theory of Locus Coeruleus-Norepinephrine Function: Adaptive Gain and Optimal Performance,” *Annual Review of Neuroscience* 28:403–450, [DOI 10.1146/annurev.neuro.28.061604.135709](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.neuro.28.061604.135709), [opened PDF](https://princetonuniversity.github.io/NEU-PSY-502/_static/pdf/Class%208/Aston-Jones2005.pdf).

**Serotonin and Doya's metalearning are contested computational hypotheses.** Doya maps dopamine to TD error δ, serotonin to temporal discount γ, noradrenaline to inverse softmax temperature β (action stochasticity), and acetylcholine to learning rate α. The mapping is proposed, not settled neuroscience; Daw, Kakade, & Dayan offer alternatives in which serotonin carries long-run reward rate and/or punishment prediction-error/opponent signals. These are learning-control parameters, not hormone amounts or emotional valence. Kenji Doya (2002), “Metalearning and Neuromodulation,” *Neural Networks* 15(4–6):495–506, §§2–3, [DOI 10.1016/S0893-6080(02)00044-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0893-6080(02)00044-8), [opened author PDF](https://people.sissa.it/~ale/EvolNeurComp/2022/II_Doya_2002.pdf); Nathaniel D. Daw, Sanjeev Kakade, & Peter Dayan (2002), “Opponent Interactions between Serotonin and Dopamine,” *Neural Networks* 15(4–6):603–616, [DOI 10.1016/S0893-6080(02)00052-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0893-6080(02)00052-7), [author PDF](https://www.princeton.edu/~ndaw/dkd02.pdf).

**Cortisol/HPA.** Cortisol is a delayed, feedback-controlled endocrine response with circadian and ultradian secretion, not a neurotransmitter-like instantaneous burst. In healthy people, most secretion occurs in hours around morning awakening; the cortisol awakening response rises during the first 30–45 min. In the original Trier Social Stress Test (10-min anticipation plus 10-min speech/arithmetic before a panel), salivary cortisol rose 2–4× baseline and peaked about 30 min after protocol onset, i.e. ~10 min after the task ended; contemporary collection protocols sample through ~90 min to establish recovery. This is a response curve, not the plasma clearance half-life. Clemens Kirschbaum, Karl-Martin Pirke, & Dirk H. Hellhammer (1993), “The Trier Social Stress Test: A Tool for Investigating Psychobiological Stress Responses in a Laboratory Setting,” *Neuropsychobiology* 28(1–2):76–81, [DOI 10.1159/000119004](https://doi.org/10.1159/000119004), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8255414/). Weitzman et al. took plasma samples every 20 min across 24 h in six healthy subjects and estimated mean cortisol decay half-life 66 min (range of secretion episodes 7–13/person). Thus 4500 s (75 min) is close to a reported clearance half-life but cannot stand in for HPA onset/peak/feedback, circadian baseline, or pulsatility. Clearance half-life H=66 min corresponds to exponential time constant τ=H/ln2≈95 min. Tobias Stalder et al. (2024), “The Cortisol Awakening Response: Regulation and Functional Significance,” *Endocrine Reviews*, [DOI 10.1210/endrev/bnae024](https://doi.org/10.1210/endrev/bnae024), [opened journal page](https://www.endocrine.org/journals/endocrine-reviews/the-cortisol-awakening-response); Martin Weitzman et al. (1971), “Twenty-Four-Hour Pattern of the Episodic Secretion of Cortisol in Normal Subjects,” *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism* 33(1):14–22, [DOI 10.1210/jcem-33-1-14](https://doi.org/10.1210/jcem-33-1-14), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4326799/).

**Epinephrine/adrenaline.** FDA prescribing information reports rapid plasma clearance after IV administration with effective half-life <5 min; pharmacology sources often report 2–3 min. Route and absorption matter, so injected IM Tmax is not endogenous stress time-to-peak. Current 120-s engineering half-life sits in the neighborhood of plasma elimination but does not model concentration, adrenal release, or how long a conversational startle changes behavior. FDA, *Epinephrine Injection* label, 2025, §12.3 [official label PDF](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2025/205029Orig1s017lbl.pdf).

**Fatigue, sleep pressure and circadian process.** Borbély's two-process model combines Process S (homeostatic sleep pressure: saturating exponential rise during wake and exponential fall during sleep) with circadian Process C (oscillator); sleep propensity depends on S+C and a circadian threshold. Standard human parameterization uses \(dS/dt=(1-S)/\tau_w\) during wake and \(dS/dt=-S/\tau_s\) during sleep, with fitted τw≈18.2 h and τs≈4.2 h in classic model applications; normalized S is 0–1. Process C is periodic and phase-linked to circadian physiology, not a day/night binary. Sleep-loss/affect coupling is context-dependent and needs its own human validation; Borbély's equations are not an affect equation. Alexander A. Borbély (1982), “A Two Process Model of Sleep Regulation. I. Physiological Basis and Outline,” *Human Neurobiology* 1:195–204 (original; no DOI verified), bibliographic record [here](https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/en/books/R100000136-I1574231874421598720); Borbély, Daan, Wirz-Justice, & Deboer (2016), “The Two-Process Model of Sleep Regulation: A Reappraisal,” *Journal of Sleep Research* 25(2):131–143, [DOI 10.1111/jsr.12371](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.12371); Daan, Beersma, & Borbély (1984), “Timing of Human Sleep: Recovery Process Gated by a Circadian Pacemaker,” *American Journal of Physiology* 246:R161–R183, [DOI 10.1152/ajpregu.1984.246.2.R161](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.1984.246.2.R161). The human parameter estimates are model-fit quantities, not universal sleep need.

**Somatic marker hypothesis.** Damasio's proposal is that learned bodily/autonomic states associated with outcomes are reactivated by imagined/considered options and bias approach/rejection; a body loop produces peripheral change and an “as-if” loop simulates it centrally. Iowa Gambling Task studies report anticipatory skin-conductance responses before disadvantageous choices in controls and impaired patterns in ventromedial prefrontal/amygdala lesion groups. This is a systems-level hypothesis, not a single accepted scalar decay equation, and causal interpretation of IGT markers is debated. Computationally, the more faithful abstraction is learned option/outcome → expected body-state association → decision bias, with physiological channels as evidence, not a generic “somatic marker” value. Antoine Bechara & Antonio R. Damasio (2005 issue; online 2004), “The Somatic Marker Hypothesis: A Neural Theory of Economic Decision,” *Games and Economic Behavior* 52:336–372, [DOI 10.1016/j.geb.2004.06.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geb.2004.06.010), [opened author PDF](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/documents/areas/fac/isrp/Bechara.G-EconBehav.pdf).

### Proposed benchmark design

1. Instrument neutralized event-level human vignettes with ratings before, immediately after, and repeated at fixed elapsed times. Record valence, arousal, dominance separately; use SAM/Affect Grid and do not infer user sentiment from the agent's current mood.
2. For event episodes, mark cause onset/end and any source reappearance; stratify by elicitor importance and initial intensity. Fit survival curves by emotion rather than a shared decay constant.
3. For mood dynamics, sample fixed intervals (e.g., 15–20 min for high-density short runs and several prompts/day for multi-day runs). Estimate person-specific OU home base/β/σ or lag-1 inertia with irregular-time handling. Never compare coefficients without Δt, scale, and estimator.
4. Test recovery after both positive and negative events and include reappraisal instruction arms; score felt experience, expression, and physiological proxy separately. A “calm output” alone cannot distinguish regulation from suppression.
5. Use clinically or ethically appropriate public datasets/consented participants for any physiological hormone outcomes. Simulated cortisol/dopamine values are not human ground truth.

### A-4 detail: arousal stored as energy

`AgentState.arousal` is a property over the `energy` backing field; the property's derived value also includes fatigue restlessness and adrenaline. Somatic updates, `apply_affect_delta`, and System-2 `apply_semantic_appraisal` write arousal through the property setter into the `energy` backing field. Since the getter first adds fatigue/adrenaline, somatic updates can bake those existing transients into energy as well. That means a short-lived signal can be absorbed into persistent base `energy` and then survive after the dopamine/adrenaline burst decays (the register's A-4). Correct design: store basal arousal separately; compute displayed/behavioral activation from basal arousal + transient channels without writing the derived value back. Keep metabolic energy/fatigue separate from PAD arousal. This makes phasic activation disappear on its own timescale and prevents it from shifting the baseline.

### Rust/Python duplicate status

`backend/app/cognitive/appraisal.py`'s `_compute_appraisal_fallback` mirrors `cognitive_rust::compute_appraisal`; normal `AppraisalEngine.appraise` calls Rust when installed. Both share A-1 because pipeline passes current agent mood as `emotional_bias`; their Jaccard novelty and deterministic acoustic heuristic are the same simplified method. `backend/app/state/agent_state.py` owns active PAD tick decay and fatigue fallback; Rust exports `update_fatigue` and `apply_alma_decay`, but only `update_fatigue` is called by production tick path. Rust's `update_pad_from_appraisal` is an exported implementation with similar weighted blend but no production callsite found; don't describe it as the active Python implementation. Its `apply_alma_decay` is divergent and should be deleted, corrected, or pinned as an exact mirror before future use.

## 4. Recommended corrections, ranked by companion benefit

1. **Make user affect reach event appraisal (A-1).** Separate user sentiment/relationship-directed affect and goal progress from current agent PAD. Expected BrainBench metric: positive-vs-negative user-turn valence delta sign agreement ≥0.9 and correlation with human turn-valence annotations; the V2 baseline is exactly 0 mood change and Cliff's δ=0. Use held-out human-labelled language/audio, not the synthetic oracle alone.
2. **Stop writing derived activation into the base state (A-4).** Separate PAD arousal, metabolic energy, fatigue, and additive phasic arousal. Metric: after a one-shot somatic/startle event, transient return to the pre-event level follows its configured time constant without residual base shift; no change to fatigue/resource interpretation after the event. Test exact equal-time decay with missing ticks.
3. **Use elapsed time for every decay (A-5).** Derive Δt from timestamps and prove partition invariance. Metrics: `1×60s` and `60×1s` produce affect values within numerical tolerance; stopped ticks still age the state by wall-clock duration. This makes existing mood and hormone time scales observable instead of scheduler-dependent.
4. **Replace “ALMA half-life” claims with explicit fitted dynamics.** Keep event emotion, mood, and relationship sentiment as distinct tiers (already accepted by DR-010), fit a per-person OU home base/return strength/variability using fixed interval human ESM or a validated longitudinal proxy. BrainBench metrics: held-out trajectory RMSE/log score, effect recovery curves, inertia by fixed Δt, and separate event/mood/relationship persistence. Avoid forcing one `λ` to satisfy minute episodes and hour/day mood.
5. **Keep neuromodulation functional and narrow.** If DA is in the name, feed it signed reward prediction error and use it to update learning/choice parameters; if cortisol is in the name, use delayed/circadian event state; if NE is introduced, test utility-sensitive phasic/tonic control and an inverted-U rather than increasing arousal monotonically. Metrics: calibration of learning/update rate, response exploration conditional on task utility, stress recovery curve—not mood valence alone.
6. **Either implement option-linked somatic memory or rename it.** Benchmark whether an associated learned consequence changes decisions in an Iowa-Gambling-like delayed-reward task or companion preference choice; current visual object warmth alone cannot validate somatic-marker claims.
7. **Do not map Process S/C to a fixed night multiplier.** Only add a sleep-pressure state if schedule, sleep/wake, and phase inputs are available. Metric: recovery after controlled sleep restriction/extension and time-of-day behavior; otherwise retain and name fatigue as an authored heuristic.
