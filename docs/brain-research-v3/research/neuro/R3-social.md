# R3 — Social, attention, initiative, and turn-taking research

**Scope.** Literature-to-code audit for social trust and closeness, mental-state inference, novelty/attention, proactive contact, conversation timing, and persona traits. This is a research report only; no implementation changes or BrainBench runs were made. Source years and DOI links below were checked against the linked publisher, PubMed/PMC, or author-hosted primary source. “Equation” is used for an equation actually specified by the cited model; conceptual frameworks without a numeric equation are identified as such.

## 1. Models

### Trust: dimensions, evidence, and repair

**Mayer, Davis & Schoorman (1995)** is a conceptual model of organizational trust, not a fitted update equation. Their Figure 1 (p. 715) puts perceived trustee **ability, benevolence, and integrity** plus the trustor’s propensity upstream of trust; perceived risk and trust jointly inform risk-taking in the relationship (RTR), and outcomes feed back into perceptions of trustworthiness. Their definition is willingness to accept vulnerability based on positive expectations of another’s actions (p. 712). Propositions 2–6 (pp. 719–728) specify relationships, not weights, units, or defaults. Ability is domain-specific. Integrity includes adherence to principles the trustor finds acceptable. Benevolence is perceived concern for the trustor. Early on, integrity is especially salient; benevolence grows in importance as the relationship develops (P3–P4). Trust should therefore predict *willingness to rely under stated stakes*, not be treated as a free-floating warmth score. [Mayer, Davis & Schoorman, “An Integrative Model of Organizational Trust,” *Academy of Management Review* 20(3), 709–734 (1995), DOI 10.5465/amr.1995.9508080335](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1995.9508080335) (Figure 1 and propositions in the [author-hosted full text](https://www.makinggood.ac.nz/media/1270/mayeretal_1995_organizationaltrust.pdf)).

This is distinct from **computational learning rules**. In the Rescorla–Wagner (RW) associative model, for a cue (i),

\[
\Delta V_i = \alpha_i\beta(\lambda - \sum_j V_j), \qquad V_{i,t+1}=V_{i,t}+\Delta V_i.
\]

Here (V_i) is associative strength (dimensionless in the model), λ is the asymptotic support from the outcome, α is cue associability and β is the outcome-specific learning-rate term; the original chapter uses empirically chosen constants for conditioning examples rather than a universal interpersonal-trust default. With one cue this is a prediction-error update; with multiple cues, the summed prediction error produces blocking/competition. The source is Rescorla & Wagner’s chapter 3, “A Theory of Pavlovian Conditioning: Variations in the Effectiveness of Reinforcement and Nonreinforcement,” pp. 64–99 in *Classical Conditioning II: Current Research and Theory* (1972), especially the model development around pp. 74–75 ([author-hosted scan](https://www.columbia.edu/~rk566/Session4/Theory%20of%20Pavlovian%20Conditioning.pdf)). It is a learning analogy, not a claim that trust equals a Pavlovian cue association.

A simpler value learner writes (V_{t+1}=V_t+\alpha\delta_t), with δ = observed outcome minus expected outcome and (0\le\alpha\le1). **Behrens et al.** used separate errors for reward and social advice fidelity and a Bayesian learner whose effective learning rate tracks source volatility; stable sources are learned slowly, changing sources quickly. They provide no universal scalar α default/range for companions. In their 24-person advice task (120 trials), advice accuracy was 75% for trials 1–30, then alternated 80% incorrect/correct every 10 trials for 40 trials, then was 85% incorrect for the final 50; participants learned advice reliability and volatility, while personal reward probabilities were separately manipulated. This gives a useful experiment design: distinguish agent competence/reliability from the *volatility* of that evidence. [Behrens, Hunt, Woolrich & Rushworth, “Associative learning of social value,” *Nature* 456, 245–249 (2008), DOI 10.1038/nature07538](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature07538) ([paper](https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/~lhunt/doc/Nature_2008_Behrens.pdf), [supplement](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1038/nature07538/MediaObjects/41586_2008_BFnature07538_MOESM306_ESM.pdf)).

**King-Casas et al.** measured a multiround trust game: trustee reciprocity strongly predicted the partner’s next-round investment; dorsal-striatal response magnitude covaried with next-play intention to trust, and the peak timing shifted by 14 seconds as reputation formed. This is evidence for a social prediction-error/reputation process in a repeated, incentivized economic game, not a 14-second trust time constant. [King-Casas et al., “Getting to know you: reputation and trust in a two-person economic exchange,” *Science* 308(5718), 78–83 (2005), DOI 10.1126/science.1108062](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1108062) ([PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15802598/)).

**Asymmetry and negative evidence need context.** Slovic’s 1993 risk-management work is the source commonly cited for public trust being damaged more by adverse than restored by comparable favorable news; its evidence concerns hypothetical news about nuclear-plant management and public confidence, not dyadic chat partners. The cited paper supplies no general numeric negative/positive learning-rate ratio. Baumeister et al.’s broad review reports “bad is stronger than good” across several domains, including relationships and feedback, but likewise does not estimate a trust-specific α ratio. More recent repeated-trust-game meta-analysis cautions against a fixed negative-learning multiplier: negative prior reputation can *reduce* updating when poor behavior confirms the prior, but *increase* revision when behavior is unexpectedly fair; interactions depend on prior manipulation and observed reciprocity. The overall meta-analytic sample included 404 effects and more than 8,000 participants across 68 studies; in the narrower moral-prior subset, reciprocity-by-negative-prior interaction was β = −2.181 (SE .912, p=.019), whereas the all-study moderation interaction was not significant. These are investment-change effect-model coefficients, **not learning rates**. [Slovic (1993), “Perceived Risk, Trust, and Democracy,” *Risk Analysis* 13(6), 675–682, DOI 10.1111/j.1539-6924.1993.tb01329.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.1993.tb01329.x); [Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Finkenauer & Vohs (2001), “Bad is stronger than good,” *Review of General Psychology* 5(4), 323–370, DOI 10.1037/1089-2680.5.4.323](https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.5.4.323); Duncan et al. (2026 issue; online 2025), “Trust learning in the repeated trust game: A meta-analytic study,” *British Journal of Psychology* 117(3), 1026–1055, DOI 10.1111/bjop.70045 ([full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13353256/)).

**Trust repair** is a sequence, not the inverse of a breach update. Lewicki & Brinsfield review breach recognition, attempted repair, and evaluation of recovery. Explanation/apology, denial, compensation, demonstrated competence, and longer-term safeguards do not work identically across breach type. Ability/competence violations are often more repairable through apology plus demonstrated competence than integrity violations; severity, intent, frequency, and attribution matter. Full restoration is not guaranteed, and recovery is under-measured. No universal repair-rate coefficient is published. [Lewicki & Brinsfield, “Trust Repair,” *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior* 4, 287–313 (2017), DOI 10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113147](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113147) (see especially pp. 288–291, 305–306; [review PDF](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-032516-113147?crawler=true&mimetype=application%2Fpdf)).

### Closeness and attachment

There is no accepted computational law that says interpersonal closeness increases linearly with trust times message count. Attachment theory describes attachment-system behavior and working models, not a scalar “attachment” accumulator. The **Inclusion of Other in the Self (IOS)** scale operationalizes subjective closeness with seven progressively overlapping-circle choices; it measures a relationship state and does not specify how fast the state should change. The self-expansion model proposes that novel, shared experiences and incorporating the other’s perspectives/resources can contribute to closeness; it is not a calibrated update rule for artificial companions. [Aron, Aron & Smollan, “Inclusion of Other in the Self Scale and the Structure of Interpersonal Closeness,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 63(4), 596–612 (1992), DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.63.4.596](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.63.4.596); [Aron & Aron, “Love and expansion of the self,” *Personal Relationships* 3(1), 45–58 (1996), DOI 10.1111/j.1475-6811.1996.tb00103.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.1996.tb00103.x).

For a companion, track separable, user-specific measures: user-stated relationship/contact preference; responsiveness as *perceived* understanding, validation, and care; shared episodes and commitments; disclosure reciprocity; recency and frequency of interaction (with exposure opportunity); trust dimensions by domain; breach/repair status; and optional user-rated closeness. A 10-year study of over 2,000 married US adults found perceived partner responsiveness predicted later eudaimonic but not hedonic wellbeing after covariates; this supports responsiveness as a useful relationship variable, not a universal chatbot target. [Selcuk, Gunaydin, Ong & Almeida, “Does Partner Responsiveness Predict Hedonic and Eudaimonic Well-being? A 10-Year Longitudinal Study,” *Journal of Marriage and Family* 78(2), 311–325 (2016), DOI 10.1111/jomf.12272](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12272). Rusbult’s investment model is another candidate for human relationships—satisfaction, alternatives, and accumulated investments predict commitment—but its romantic-relationship evidence should not be relabeled as an AI-friend formula. [Rusbult, “Commitment and satisfaction in romantic associations: A test of the investment model,” *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 16(2), 172–186 (1980), DOI 10.1016/0022-1031(80)90007-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031(80)90007-4).

### Theory of mind and affect inference from language

Human mental-state inference is better described as inference from multiple, uncertain cues than as direct access to a partner’s state. Words can explicitly report feelings/goals, but otherwise their meaning depends on conversational context, shared knowledge, behavior, and (in speech) prosody, timing, and visible cues. A Bayesian pragmatic framing treats the listener as comparing candidate speaker goals/beliefs that could have generated an utterance; it does not yield a single universal valence-from-text equation or human accuracy band. Experimental emotion-inference research finds context can predict judgments as well as, or better than, isolated facial information, with cue integration varying by emotion and observer. Thus for text-only dialogue, benchmark *calibration and uncertainty* against the speaker’s own later report; do not take an annotator or model label as ground truth. For a general methods review see [Apperly, “Theory of mind: mechanisms, methods, and new directions,” *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 7:413 (2013), DOI 10.3389/fnhum.2013.00413](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2013.00413); [Goel, Jara-Ettinger, Ong & Gendron, “Face and context integration in emotion inference is limited and variable across categories and individuals,” *Nature Communications* 15, 2443 (2024), DOI 10.1038/s41467-024-46670-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-46670-5).

In richer language tasks, human participants can infer referential intent and what a speaker knows from word choice; computational Bayesian ToM work formalizes this as latent-agent inference. Jara-Ettinger & Rubio-Fernandez model the posterior as

\[
p(t,b\mid u) \propto \sum_{r\in[0,1]}p(u\mid t,b,r)p(r)p(t\mid b)p(b),
\]

where $t$ is intended referent, $b$ the speaker’s belief/visible-object subset, $r$ the speaker-specific tendency to use redundant words, and $u$ the utterance (Eq. 1, “Computational framework,” p. 2). Their experiments use uniform $p(b)$ and $p(t\mid b)$ on controlled visual scenes; the redundancy prior is Beta-distributed, with $B(.39,.32)$ and $B(.32,1.12)$ in experiments 1 and 2. The model fit participants’ mean belief judgments at $r=.95$ (95% CI .92–.98) in experiment 1; in experiment 2, participants adjusted their belief inferences to speaker-specific redundancy, and the model fit at $r=.85$ (95% CI .71–1.00). These are task-specific correlation fits, not general emotion inference accuracy. A sensible repeated-interaction learner stores claims with provenance/confidence and updates beliefs only when new utterances or outcomes bear on them. User self-report should supersede a prior inference; confidence should fall when cues conflict. [Jara-Ettinger & Rubio-Fernandez, “Quantitative mental state attributions in language understanding,” *Science Advances* 7(47), eabj0970 (2021), DOI 10.1126/sciadv.abj0970](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abj0970) ([PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8597992/)). Current code’s LLM-emitted valence/arousal/goals are not evidence that the system’s inferences are calibrated.

### Novelty, orienting, and habituation

**Sokolov’s comparator account** says the orienting response follows mismatch between incoming input and a learned neural model; repeated standards become represented and cease to elicit the same response, while a changed stimulus renews orienting. The source is a conceptual/neurophysiological account, not a scalar text novelty equation with fitted defaults. The 1963 book and Sokolov’s 1963 review are separate sources: E. N. Sokolov, *Perception and the Conditioned Reflex* (Pergamon Press, 1963), and “Higher Nervous Functions: The Orienting Reflex,” *Annual Review of Physiology* 25, 545–580 (1963), DOI 10.1146/annurev.ph.25.030163.002553 ([publisher DOI](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.ph.25.030163.002553)).

Thompson & Spencer’s habituation review identifies response decrement, often approximately negative-exponential over repeated stimulus presentations, plus spontaneous recovery, stimulus specificity/generalization, dishabituation, and rate/intensity effects. Rankin et al. revise the behavioral characteristics and add long-term habituation; neither provides one species- and stimulus-independent decay constant. The competing Groves–Thompson dual-process account separates habituation from sensitization, allowing an intense event to transiently raise response while habituation remains. Those distinctions imply that conversational novelty should decline for *repeated equivalent content*, recover with elapsed time/context change, generalize by semantic similarity, and rise after meaningful unexpected information; novelty and emotional arousal should not be identical. [Thompson & Spencer, “Habituation: A model phenomenon for the study of neuronal substrates of behavior,” *Psychological Review* 73(1), 16–43 (1966), DOI 10.1037/h0022681](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0022681) (p. 18 and characteristics 1–9); [Rankin et al., “Habituation Revisited: An Updated and Revised Description of the Behavioral Characteristics of Habituation,” *Neurobiology of Learning and Memory* 92(2), 135–138 (2009), DOI 10.1016/j.nlm.2008.09.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nlm.2008.09.012) ([open full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2754195/)).

**Bayesian surprise** gives a computationally explicit alternative. Itti & Baldi define information gained by updating a model after data (D) as

\[
S(D;M)=D_{KL}\!\left(P(M\mid D)\,\|\,P(M)\right)
=\int_M P(M\mid D)\log\frac{P(M\mid D)}{P(M)}\,dM.
\]

This is zero when observation leaves the model unchanged; using natural log gives nats, log base 2 gives bits. It is the divergence between prior and posterior beliefs, **not** merely −log likelihood of the message. Their visual implementation uses a Gamma prior over Poisson rates; shape α and inverse-scale β are updated with event count (k), forgetting factor (f=.7) in that visual model. In a natural-video gaze experiment with 8 naïve observers and 50 clips (~25 minutes), 72% of gaze shifts landed at locations above-average Bayesian surprise (84% for consensus locations). Those are visual-attention data; using semantic KL for text is a motivated transfer hypothesis, not a published text effect size. [Itti & Baldi, “Bayesian surprise attracts human attention,” *Vision Research* 49(10), 1295–1306 (2009), DOI 10.1016/j.visres.2008.09.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2008.09.007) (Eq. 4, p. 1296; visual experiment pp. 1302–1304; [author copy](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/classes/fa09/cse87-b/cogs200-f09/readings/itti-baldi-2009.pdf)).

### Initiative and intentions

**Prospective memory** concerns remembering to perform an intended action in the future. Einstein & McDaniel’s multiprocess account distinguishes strategic monitoring from spontaneous cue-triggered retrieval; demanding continuous monitoring creates ongoing-task costs, while salient, distinctive cues can trigger retrieval more automatically. A companion should therefore represent *user-authorized* intentions with cue/deadline, confidence, importance, and expiry, then surface them when context/time makes them useful—not generate outreach simply because the system is idle. The source is a theory/review, not a social-contact-frequency model. [Einstein & McDaniel, “Prospective Memory: Multiple Retrieval Processes,” *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 14(6), 286–290 (2005), DOI 10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00382.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00382.x); for experiments distinguishing monitoring from spontaneous retrieval, [Einstein et al., “Multiple processes in prospective memory retrieval,” *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 134(3), 327–342 (2005), DOI 10.1037/0096-3445.134.3.327](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.134.3.327).

Christoff et al.’s spontaneous-thought framework treats mind-wandering as relatively unconstrained transitions among thoughts, constrained by deliberate goals and automatic salience/affect; the default network can contribute variability, with salience, attention, and control networks shaping constraints. It is a dynamic neurocognitive framework, not evidence that a companion has a literal subconscious or should contact a user. [Christoff, Irving, Fox, Spreng & Andrews-Hanna, “Mind-wandering as spontaneous thought: a dynamic framework,” *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 17, 718–731 (2016), DOI 10.1038/nrn.2016.113](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn.2016.113).

RW/TD-style extinction says expected action value should decline when an action repeatedly fails to earn its expected outcome; define reward from user response in advance and distinguish no response (censored/ambiguous) from explicit dismissal. The current W9 rule makes three *recorded ignores* close re-raise probability to zero, a hand-set policy, not a learned RW/TD value. There is no evidence-backed universal rate for unprompted companion messages per day/week or a universally acceptable send time. Social appropriateness is user-, channel-, and context-specific; treat explicit settings and the user’s empirical active-hour/contact-response distribution as the control signals, with opt-out and quiet hours as hard constraints. Do not turn a sparse log of received messages into a learned permission to increase frequency.

### Turn-taking, interruption, and grounding

For spontaneous face-to-face conversation, Stivers et al. measured polar question/answer transitions in ten languages. Across the sample, turn offset’s **mode was 0 ms**, median −100 ms, and mean −208 ms (negative means overlap); language means ranged from −7 ms (Japanese) to −469 ms (Danish), with all within ±250 ms of the overall mean. Confirmation answers were 100–500 ms faster than disconfirmations in each language. These data describe spoken question/answer sequences, not text-chat delay. Levinson’s review summarizes average turn length near 2 s, modal gap ~200 ms and modal overlap under 100 ms; fewer than 5% of the speech stream has simultaneous speakers. Use distributions conditioned on language, activity, and response type, rather than a single human pause target. [Stivers et al., “Universals and cultural variation in turn-taking in conversation,” *PNAS* 106(26), 10587–10592 (2009), DOI 10.1073/pnas.0903616106](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903616106) ([full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2705608/)); [Levinson, “Turn-taking in human communication, origins, and implications for language processing,” *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 20(1), 6–14 (2016), DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2015.10.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2015.10.010) ([author PDF](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2193297_10/component/file_2240007/Levinson_TICS_2016.pdf)).

Levelt’s analysis of 959 spontaneous self-repairs describes monitoring, interruption/hesitation/editing, then repair; speakers interrupt promptly after detecting trouble, except they tend to finish a correct word. Clark & Brennan treat grounding as reaching sufficient mutual understanding using available evidence, with requirements shaped by communication medium. For an assistant, interruption success is not just stop latency: preserve what was actually heard, acknowledge uncertainty, allow correction, and resume only after grounding. [Levelt, “Monitoring and self-repair in speech,” *Cognition* 14(1), 41–104 (1983), DOI 10.1016/0010-0277(83)90026-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0277(83)90026-4) ([author PDF](https://www.mpi.nl/world/materials/publications/Levelt/1983_Levelt.pdf)); [Clark & Brennan, “Grounding in Communication,” in *Perspectives on Socially Shared Cognition*, pp. 127–149 (1991), DOI 10.1037/10096-006](https://doi.org/10.1037/10096-006) ([author PDF](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~illah/CLASSDOCS/Clark91.pdf)).

Voice activity projection and turn-end prediction are engineering models for estimating whether the current speaker will continue and when a transition-relevance place is likely; the above interactional results constrain distributions but do not prescribe a universal VAP architecture or weights. The repo’s static affect-weighted initiation probability is not a VAP model and is not fitted to conversational pause data.

### Personality as a dynamic distribution

Big Five and HEXACO are trait taxonomies measured by inventories; the taxonomies alone do not provide a turn-by-turn policy equation. Fleeson’s three experience-sampling studies found high within-person variability: people routinely expressed nearly all levels of trait-relevant behavior over 2–3 weeks, while individual means were highly stable; variability itself also differed reliably between people. Whole Trait Theory combines the descriptive distribution (mean, variability, skew, kurtosis) with social-cognitive mechanisms that make behavior contingent on situation. It therefore supports a *stable tendency plus state/context reaction*, not a permanent adjective-to-response mapping. [Fleeson, “Toward a structure- and process-integrated view of personality: Traits as density distributions of states,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 80(6), 1011–1027 (2001), DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.80.6.1011](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.80.6.1011); [Fleeson & Jayawickreme, “Whole Trait Theory,” *Journal of Research in Personality* 56, 82–92 (2015), DOI 10.1016/j.jrp.2014.10.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrp.2014.10.009).

### Why the measured novelty result is inverted

The code’s subtraction is pointed in the conventional direction: high token overlap lowers its score. Yet the benchmark’s repeated-tag mean is higher. That establishes that the score is not measuring the benchmark’s *semantic* repetition construct in these runs. With only the last 20 messages and only the first 100 characters retained, a tagged repeat can fall outside the lookup window or differ lexically after paraphrase/truncation, while a “fresh” same-topic event can overlap heavily with a recent message and score lower. The aggregate can therefore reverse even when each comparison behaves as written. The evaluator uses chronological simulation turns and tags from lifesim annotations; the next diagnostic should join each repeated turn to its antecedent and log Jaccard, antecedent age, retained-window membership, full vs truncated text, and semantic event IDs. Until that trace is run, “history/label or aggregation mismatch” is a diagnosis to test, not a proven unique root cause.

## 4. Recommended corrections, ranked by companion benefit

1. **Stop trust saturation and neutral-turn credit (highest benefit).** Gate trust evidence on an attributable behavior/outcome; keep ability, benevolence, integrity separate; represent uncertainty and stakes; keep repair type-aware. **BrainBench metrics:** background mean trust delta should approach zero; ceiling fraction should remain low enough that hostility response stays measurable; trust change should be monotone with attributed reliability and breach severity; reliability calibration by domain. Baseline is +.0411 trust/ordinary turn, integrity ceiling in 6 turns, competence in 13, and all hostility masked at ceiling.
2. **Make repetition detectable over semantic content and time.** First instrument the benchmark antecedent/window relation; then replace token-set novelty with semantic/event-level predictive surprise plus habituation/recovery. **Metrics:** positive ordering (repeated < fresh), positive sign under the suite’s exact Cliff’s-δ convention, near-duplicate collision rate below the V2 3.6–4.6%, and controlled decay/recovery/dishabituation curves. Preserve semantic novelty after paraphrase and avoid declaring every wording variation surprising.
3. **Bring proactive contact under user-level control and outcome learning.** Treat deadlines/commitments as prospective memories; explicit contact preference/quiet time beats inferred routine; distinguish silence from dismissal; impose absolute per-day caps and stable cooldown/watermark behavior. **Metrics:** useful-contact rate up; annoyance, unsolicited contacts/day, night fraction, dismissals, and cooldown violations down; stratify by user archetype and stated preference. V2’s `none` sync already yields ~20 attempts/day and ~20 annoyance/day; broadcast reset causes >1,200/day. This is likely more important than increasing biological fidelity of an “idle thought” module.
4. **Calibrate user affect/goal inference and expose uncertainty.** Maintain a source-linked, decaying mental-state hypothesis with explicit self-report correction; avoid treating inferred valence as fact. **Metrics:** Brier/log loss, calibration error, correction uptake, and downstream helpfulness against subsequent user self-report; include sarcasm/ambiguous/context-only examples.
5. **Separate companion initiative from speech turn-taking.** Rename static affect score to an initiative prior. Use actual VAD/turn-final/endpoint confidence and language/user-specific gap distributions for voice; maintain lifecycle and heard-history invariants. **Metrics:** interruption stop latency, false barge-in, overlap/gap percentiles, zero-terminal replies, and history-equals-heard rate.
6. **Represent persona as traits × situation, not a list of adjectives.** Keep immutable user-authored character; measure tendencies and variance across situation classes; adapt only permitted relationship/style fields. **Metrics:** blind trait-consistency ratings alongside situational appropriateness, within-person response distributions, user correction rate, and relationship-specific preference fit.

### Verification limits

The report’s human benchmarks come from task-specific studies and reviews. None validates the project’s bespoke scalar ranges as human parameters. Trust-game investments, public risk trust, romantic closeness, visual gaze and spoken turn offsets are different constructs and should remain separately labeled in BrainBench. The novelty inversion’s exact root cause, and the translation from any cited finding into companion contact preferences, require targeted turn-level instrumentation and user-derived data respectively.
